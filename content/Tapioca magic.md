There is this insane experience that I get to have anytime I want, in the comfort of my home, and it's making a tapioca. 

Basically, I pour flour into a pan to form a thin layer, and out of nowhere, the flour sticks together, stops being flour, and starts being a pancake.

I hope to research this more in the near future and note down what in the physics wizardry happens in that pan. Though I suppose it's hardly related scientifically, affectively the process feels like the intersection of [non-newtonian fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Newtonian_fluid) and, well, pancakes.

# Culinary and linguistic origins

First of all, you might have already heard the word tapioca before. 
I heard it first during the 2010s, right in the German hype phase of bubble tea. In that period, the texture-wise rather controversial black *tapioca pearls* stuck to people's gums as much as to public property. I miss those days.

Anyway, those pearls are made from the same thing as these pancakes, which is essentially just processed starch. This starch comes from *mandioca*, the manioc root (also known as cassava in English, yucca en Español, and a bunch of other names). Though hardly known in Germany beyond those sticky pearls, it is the [third most importance source of carbohydrates in the tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassava), right after rice and corn! 

According to [this paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1878450X21001165), the word tapioca is [[Portuguese, Pretoguese, and the colonization of language|borrowed from Brazilian indigenous languages]]:

*"In the literature, there are two different origins for the word tapioca, both from indigenous culture. One comes from the term tïpï'og, which in Tupi, an indigenous language of the Northeast of Brazil, means “sediment” (Bacelar and Góis, 1997). It refers to the sedimented starchy mass that is obtained in the process of starch manufacturing. From another perspective, according to Bezerra (2005), the word tapioca is translated by the indigenous people as “house bread” (tapi = bread and oca = house). Its current meaning has thus remained quite coherent to the cultural tradition of family consumption of tapioca."*



# Tapiocarization, or: Chemistry for dummies

My last chemistry class happened more than 10 years ago, so I do not guarantee for factual correctness. That being said, I am going to attempt to describe the chemical process of *tapiocarization* in two stages:
1. Powder goes sauce (aka, starch gelatinization)
2. Sauce goes solid (aka, sintering)


## Stage 1: Powder goes sauce

In stage 1, what happens is exactly the same process that occurs when you put starch into water and heat it up: You make sauce (aka a gooey, viscous hybrid between solid and liquid). At room temperature, the starch is simply suspended in the water, floating around aimlessly. Adding heat causes the water to penetrate the starch granules, which in consequence, swell up and break down. Water + starch + heat = sauce.

The crazy difference with tapiocas is that the water *is already inside the starch*. The starch is hydrated to like 37%, with the water being absorbed in and between the starch particles. I guess this is also why the granules already lump together more or less strongly when they first enter the pan!
Now, the heat makes this already contained water wiggle around more, liberating it so that it moves into different molecular regions of the starch. There, it sabotages the neatly-organized arrangement of glucose chains, and eventually, breaks down the structure. The result of this is the classic gel-like consistency of sauce.

But hold up, why don't we see tapioca goo flow around in the pan then? Enter phase 2: goo goes solid (aka, sintering).

## Stage 2: Sauce goes solid

In fact, the mixture does actually have this saucey kind of consistency–at least temporarily, and only *inside* the pancake. But then why do we only see pancake? The reason is "[sintering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintering)", or the half-melting of any material that makes parts of it stick together and form a solid.

The above described creation of sticky gel (gel-atinization) acts like glue between neighboring particles. The starch granule surfaces "melt", they become sticky and form bridges between one another. However, the intense heat from the pan causes the bottom of the pancake to behave differently: The water evaporates soon after beginning gelatinization, leaving behind a larger solid mass that has been fused together.

Inside the tapioca, it's a little less hot, and water is trapped more effectively. Therefore, it has a harder time escaping, and gelatinization goes on for longer, leaving the core of the pancake more gooey. At the air-exposed top, the water can evaporate easily, drying up the saucing-process. 



## Non-newtonian pancakes?

So, a bunch of absurd things happen during tapiocarization.

There is a powder that I put into my pan, which is both dry and wet at the same time. 
When my stove provides heat, this dry-wet powder melts into an imperceptible sauce. 
At the same time as this sauce is forming in front of me, it is already turning into a solid again.
Basically, me, my pan and my tapioca powder form a forbidden alchemist.

When I first encountered tapiocas, just because of these weirdnesses, I had to think of non-newtonian fluids: the kinds of fluid that don't behave like they are supposed to. Where water always behaves like water–for example, flowing faster as you push it harder–this atypical category of fluids *changes* its viscosity, only flowing under slow movement and stiffening under pressure. As it happens, tapiocas kind of ARE the intersection of non-newtonian fluids and, well, pancakes! After all, the most famous non-newtonian fluid ([oobleck](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/mtr1s3z724o)) is a starch-water mix, which is exactly what a tapioca is.

So, does that mean that the gelatinized center (the more liquid part) of a tapioca pancake would behave like one? Apart from the obviously challenging extraction of just the center part of a tapioca (maybe if you scaled it up immensely?), there is another unlucky issue. Oobleck only behaves so weirdly because the starch granules are still suspended in the water, jamming together when exposed to force. As we have learned, the heat during tapiocarization does away with this suspension. So, unfortunately, even if you somehow managed to extract only the pancake's center en masse, you'd just be left with, well, goo.
