## Cultural clocks

In São Paulo, busses routinely drag behind schedule half an hour and more. At times, I have waited for a bus for over an hour, just to give up eventually because it never came. 
Students (and insanely, ALSO PROFESSORS) shamelessly arrive a whole hour late to class. 
Rain has been a reason for people to cancel plans. Even though the plans were to meet somewhere inside.
Cashiers have eternal chill. They boop the products over the counter like sloths. Sometimes they are so slow because they are having a more or less fun conversation. But sometimes I just cannot pick out any reason for their sluggishness, and when I am really hungry, it becomes a challenging exercise in patience.

What do all of these situations have in common? They all go to show how Brazil runs on a different clock. Brazilian time.

So what, then, is German time? (Besides 4-5 hours ahead?) 
The infamous myth of German punctuality, of course, could not be farther from the truth. Trains in Germany are famously late (or cancelled) more often than not. When I meet with friends, I frequently see them a lot later than initially planned. Big infrastructure projects like Stuttgart 21 or the Berlin Brandenburg Airport are decades late in opening. Or take the humble doctor's appointment: You book your appointment in January, get a slot in May, and finally arrive perfectly on time for it only to wait there a whole hour because, as you had already expected, the doctor, too, is running behind.

However, the most interesting disparity between German and Brazilian time is not so much the differing amount of minutes it might take for something to happen in each respective culture. In fact, this may or may not vary that much at all. Instead, the interesting part of varying ✨ cultural temporalities ✨ actually lies in expectation management.


## Being sharp about being late

Even though German trains are never on time, *this is always communicated*. Badly, it goes without saying, but it is: On the platform, the ever-waiting passenger routinely hears the familiar voices echo excuses that range anywhere from "operational issues" to "technical malfunctions". What do all these announcements actually tell you? Perhaps their intention is to enhance credibility–I heard once that giving *any* kind of reason makes people agree more–and thereby aim to appease passengers' rage.

But much more than that, the reason might be that German culture simply expects it. 
Being late is normal only to the point that it occurs often–not to the point where it is assumed to be okay. Hating on the German railway company has already become a national sport, and exactly for this reason. Delays are not only noticed, but also perceived as being, after all this time, still noteworthy. Despite their factual proliferation, delays are regarded as unacceptable, something that is, even in face of decades of experience, still worth commenting on. In small talk as much as in platform announcements. The latter need not even be reliable: In Germany, a train will first be announced as five minutes late, then 10, then 30, and then, after an hour of waiting, as cancelled. As if the delay accumulated one honest minute at a time rather than existing from the start. But! At least there will always be a notification on some display informing you about the inconvenient development.

Conversely, in São Paulo, you may wait ages for a bus that never comes. And crucially: No one will tell you that it will never come. When I meet a friend in Germany, I will let them know I will be late, even if it is just a few minutes. This would seem ridiculous in Brazil: Arriving anywhere up to like 20 minutes later is simply still perceived as on time.


## Scarcity or abundance? Circumstances or character?

In Germany I get the impression that there is never enough time for things. I rush from one thing to the next, hurrying to minimize the social sanction I already expect to be exposed to. In Brazil, there is time. Let that sink in–that one sentence that almost therapeutically calms me down when I remind myself of it: *There is time.* 

There is simply enough time. Finish what you are doing in peace, don't run to the next thing yet, have that conversation, tudo tranquilo, the customer waiting in line can stay a waiting customer for a moment more. As idealized and romanticized as this will sound, I feel that Brazil just puts that one extra pinch of appreciation on the present moment.

Hand in hand with this goes, almost by necessity, that being on time is *a matter of the circumstances*. Maybe the bus driver's cigarettes had run out before his shift this morning, so he had to buy a new pack. Consequently, all of today's rides were a little delayed. Since the bus driver was late, so was the student. The professor took an [[Uber & class|Uber]] to uni, but the Uber got stuck in traffic–no problem, the seminar simply begins once enough people had trickled into the lecture hall. As it happens, the cashier is new to this particular store, and his coworker is giving him instructions on finding the codes for certain veggies, as the line of customers slowly lengthens.

In contrast, being on time in Germany is a *matter of moral character*, of integrity, of virtue. Accordingly, the bus driver should have noticed his predicament last night and taken appropriate action. The student should have simply taken an earlier bus, and the professor, knowing her daily route, ought to know to leave the house with a buffer to make up for the well-known traffic around that hour. Failing to do so is simply poor planning, not bad luck. Similarly, the cashier in training should have studied the register properly before serving real customers. He is being paid to work, a queue is a queue, and its patience is his responsibility.


## So?

Embracing Brazilian time has been refreshing and relaxing: I am frequently late to things and enjoy the process. Even having to wait for people has something oddly enjoyable to it: Because you don't plan for it, you're forced to make something of the moment you are presented with. A chance to finally look properly at that street that you've walked on so many times but have never given much attention. A little [vai-tomar-no-cu](https://translate.google.com/?sl=pt&tl=en&text=vai-tomar-no-cu&op=translate) in the face of neoliberal self-optimization. On the other hand, I also like how German culture values other people's time. It makes sense to me to understand it as a scarce resource and assigning value to treating it with care. In the end, it's not like I can simply live by my own preference anyway, what matters more is the cultural embedding surrounding me. But the experience of living in another such surrounding for a while surely helps to take some healthy distance from the ostensibly fixed rules of any cultural context.
