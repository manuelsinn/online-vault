The University of São Paulo (USP) is a village. And a city. But mostly a village. And that's amazing.

The campus is separated from the rest of the city by a fence and guarded *portarias*, which together encircle what is literally called *Cidade Universitária*. Within this *university city*, there is pretty much anything you need: Bakeries, restaurants, laundry mats, libraries, gyms, music rooms, and a huge sports complex that houses even an Olympic swimming pool. (With a whole diving tower.) It’s so big that there exist *five different bus lines* connecting the different corners of the campus. 

USP is grande not only spatially but also in terms of things to do: There are hundreds of societies, clubs, and sports teams, which also compete against one another and against other universities regularly. Curiously, this also makes everything here feel oddly American—people even proudly rep USP sweaters or shirts from their faculties, everywhere. Friends I’ve met here do everything from rowing to cheerleading to jiujitsu, play instruments in orchestras, or are super engaged in university politics. The huge [[parties on campus]] also bring high-key college vibes (even though they lack the red cups).

One of my favorite places on campus, the DCE student space, is regularly filled with an absurd variety of activities. When I go there, I variably see people eating lunch, practicing violin, wrestling on the ground in BJJ training or assuming crazy body poses in Kung Fu. One evening, I even stumbled over a full-out boxing match, so I cancelled my evening plans to feel the unexpected intensity of accompanying people hitting each other. 

At the same time, it feels super small: After a while you always frequent the same areas, you get to know shortcuts along the small pathways in between buildings, internalize the bike lane network, and most importantly for the village vibes, constantly bump into students you’ve already met, most of all in the [[queueing|cafeteria queue]].

This village-like ecosystem is a welcome, or rather, much needed, contrast to the city’s overwhelming intensity. It enables life in São Paulo to be both full of immense diversity and cultural offers and at the same time offers a degree of community (actually, even a meta community, a community of communities) that seems easily lost or hard to find otherwise, in the grand anonymity of existing as just one speck in between 11 million others. Living in this context, I understand (once more) [[The architecture of a good life, or - Why is university people’s favorite period?|why university is people’s favorite period in life]]. 

It provides an ecosystem so complete that a significant part of students hardly ever leaves the campus. How must it be to then graduate for these people? What a strange kind of loss, leaving your life behind in which you had friends, connections, structures, communities, validation and recognition in spaces all intimately connected, only to then be exposed to the violence and anonymity of the "real world"—all without ever actually leaving physically?

This community of communities being the central anchor of life as an intercâmbista makes everything feel a lot more connected, easy, and just so rich. I’ve made [[Sidequests taken on in Brazil|good use of these opportunities]] and still there are so many things I want to try as well, from playing Go at the architecture faculty to developing my analog films in the visual arts department, e muito mais. 

Anyway. I guess I’m not saying we all need to live in a commune. But maybe like a community of communities?
