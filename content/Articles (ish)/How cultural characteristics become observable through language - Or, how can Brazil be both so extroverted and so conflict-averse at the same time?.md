Brazil is the extrovert's paradise. I've had people approach me in all kinds of situations. At parties, on the street, in the supermarket, in museums. I've even had strangers at the bus station ask me where I was going, giving me advice on routes, and paying for my bus ticket. Multiple times. So, naturally, in the case of a conflict, I expected people to come up to me in their outgoing manner, be super friendly and then casually and openly talk about the problem at hand.

However, something like this never happened. I've hardly ever witnessed direct conflict here (except maybe with the [[Brazilian police|police]]) and open confrontation seems like it's just not a thing. For example, whenever I encountered some issue at home (say, flatmates who left the common areas unusable, [[it has rained from the ceiling|rooms that fell short of their marketed promises]], or a [[colagem de cocô (explicit)|doggo doing his business where I wanted to cook]]), I never heard any of my flatmates lose a single critical word about the situation. This left me a bit confused: How can a culture that is so outgoing, warm, talkative and inviting, at the same time be so indirect, even conflict-averse?

Okay - I have heard one critical word perhaps, which you may encounter frequently in Brazil. "Esquisito" is a classic false friend, I thought it meant *exquisite*, magnificent, elegant, tasteful. The rarity that comes with that understanding holds true, but its connotation is the opposite: It means odd, weird, or strange. I feel it's used in exactly these kinds of situations, where there is a dislikable situation, and this word comes in handy to describe that situation without appearing overly critical. Which seems to be a common theme.

## It's complicated :P 

Often when I talk with Brazilians about things that (to me) are outright shit - that make me angry, sad, annoyed or invoke any other unpleasant reaction - I yearn for someone to call it by its name. I thirst for people to express their discontent. What I hear instead in many cases is the phrase "tá complicado" (it's complicated). And it leaves me RAGING! Aren't some things simply unjust, depressing, and annoying? And I am not even talking about disagreements and multiperspectivity, of course there are different angles to issues. But even if someone agrees with me, they would say, well yeah... it's complicated.

## Do you accept sugar?

Another, more innocent example. I have been asked the question "aceita açucar?" (do you accept sugar?). What may sound a little strange at least and could at most be misunderstood as an inquiry about one's argumentative stance on the stimulating substance is, of course, just a simple offer that follows a serving of coffee.

I find this curious because in this scenario, to ask if the someone might *accept* something that is offered seems like an inversion of responsibility: As if the sole act of *affirming that you would like something* (not even voicing it by yourself, mind you) is already a burden that one should avoid placing on someone else's shoulders. Instead, the interaction is framed as if the waiter were to bring the coffee, the sugar, the water to the guest *anyway*, and the guest is simply tasked with *accepting* it, reducing any possible inconvenience to the minimum. 

## Too much!

Though there is a word for "too much" (demais), I hear people use other expressions much more. To express that you find something to be "too expensive" for example, you might just say "muito caro" (or, if you feel strongly about it, *muuuito caro*). If you want to express the same thing in a more indirect way, you can also say kind of the *opposite*: "esse restaurante é *um pouco* caro" (this restaurant is *a bit* expensive). All of this makes sense and feels intuitive to a large degree. But for me, there is still a semantic difference between "very expensive" and "too expensive". Aren't they just not the same??

## Interesting...

When I asked strangers about the security of a neighborhood and whether they deem it necessary to order an Uber to get to the bus station, they told me: "Seria interessante pegar um Uber" (it would be interesting to take an Uber). Obviously the guy didn't talk about Uber rides being intellectually stimulating. Rather, he framed his advice (which I even asked for directly!) in a super indirect way. Consider this spectrum: 

| Unnecessarily confrontational | Are you my parent?       | Friendly support                    | Elaborate manipulation scheme                  | ??? |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --- |
| You must take an Uber!        | You should take an Uber. | It would be good to take an Uber... | It would be ✨ interesting ✨ to take an Uber... | ??? |

(To be fair, I guess it's kind of like "it wouldn't be a bad idea to take an Uber at this time of night" which is arguably even more indirect.)



# So?

At times I felt like this indirect manner of expression has an unauthentic taste to it. Something that keeps people at a distance from one another, as if a speakers somehow distrusts that they may place their discontent in the ears of a listener without fear of negative repercussions. For a moment I was even thinking, could the country's authoritarian history have left a mark on its language, inscribing in it the once justified fear of positioning oneself?

Now I think that indirect and direct communication are pretty equally capable of getting information across. Just that the indirectness comes with a bunch of extra cues, which, if you have a hard time reading them (like me), might slightly distort the meaning of the message. 

Importantly, the tendency towards friendly conversationalism might not actually stand in such a strong opposition to conflict aversion and addressing problems indirectly: In the end, both inclinations foster lighthearted good-vibes relationships. After all, it wouldn't be the first time that the ideal of harmony functions as a kind of societal lubricant, working towards cooperation and stability.

Of course you can accuse Brazilians of not really caring how you're actually doing when they whip out a casual *tudo bem* (not unlike Americans, with their disingenuous *how are you*). But in a way, that would just be misinterpreting the function of that little piece of communication, taking it a bit too seriously, too much at face value. Obviously, changing your mind about it is a little easier than changing your affective reaction to it. But isn't that a nice reminder of how much we're all stuck in our own respective culturalization. 

That being said, the expression "tá complicado" has actually been growing on me recently: I've developed more of a feeling for its weight, which some might argue might even increase through the additional burden of not naming the cause for disliking something. As if both interlocutors shared the knowledge of how severe something is, and perhaps even a similar conviction on what the cause for it is, yet need not (cannot?) speak it out loud.
