In Brazil, Uber rides are cheap. And convenient. And abundant.
The attractivity of simply taking a 10-minute Uber to skip a one-hour bus or bike ride, for just a few euros, is hard to abstain from. And in some places, like Rio, you can opt for the even cheaper and [[Jeitinho Brasileiro, or a list of things that lend themselves to romantization#Moto Ubers|time-saving adrenaline rush on a motorcycle]]. On the other hand, I notice how rides add up fast, and I've been wondering who can really afford them. And what role Uber plays in the city.

## Why is Uber cheap in Brazil?

While I sometimes find Uber prices ridiculously cheap comparing them to German taxi rides, I realize the skewedness of this privileged perspective. Nonetheless, lower local wages mean that the minimum a driver will accept per ride is also lower, resulting in prices that Brazilians can pay. Interestingly, another influence to pricing is the fact that Uber literally subsidizes fares to flood streets and grab market share!

Most importantly however, the supposedly cheap prices are squeezed out of workers, who work longer and longer hours for the marginal profit per ride to add up to a minimal salary. Precarity is only exacerbated by [surging international fuel prices and a weakening Brazilian currency](https://www.vice.com/en/article/uber-drivers-in-brazil-are-struggling-to-break-even-on-rides/). Therefore, when I look at Uber cars swarming around the map as I wait for a ride, I can't help to think of them as pawns moving around the board, subject to players out of sight who determine their circumstances. 


## (Sidequest: Scammy icons and platform capitalism)

As a friend needed to point out to me, this depiction of cars on the app is itself just another malicious upselling strategy: It suggests availability where there is none, driving you towards impatience and thereby to accepting higher prices for a shorter wait. This leaves me thinking that, to the company itself, the physical presence or absence of drivers in any specific moment is entirely secondary. It is not commited to providing truthful information–the precarious context effectively ensures the ample availability of nearby cars at any time, so their real physical locations can be ignored in favor of presenting a more profitable reality.

Though this is clearly not the biggest issue, it still serves as a reminder to how (badly) the platforms we use on a daily basis treat those who are contracted by them (ahem, Spotify?). Uber romantically frames itself as a neutral mediator between those who seek a ride and those who are willing to offer it, supposedly only providing the convenient marketplace for this matchmaking. When you think about it (like the working conditions and profit distribution) for too long, this framing becomes an almost cynical distortion of the truth.


## Who actually uses Uber?

Prices "Brazilians" can pay, of course, refers only to those Brazilians who can afford to use Uber routinely. But who is that? A typical Uber in São Paulo runs R$12–25 (between 2 and 5 euros) depending on distance and demand, so R$18 (3€) might be a reasonable midpoint. "Routine use" (using it for going out, errands, avoiding the metro at night) comes out to maybe 20 rides/month, so around R$360 (60€). It seems reasonable to assume people do not spend more than 10% for discretionary transport (and probably less - São Paulo is expensive). That would mean that routine Uber use is viable for incomes over R$3600 (600€). 

That figure sharply contrasts the minimum wage in São Paulo, which is merely R$1874, around 316€. It would require at least twice that amount to reach a routine-uber-use-income. Meanwhile, more than [1.7 million inhabitants live in São Paulo's favelas](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/cotidiano/2024/11/favelas-de-sao-paulo-tem-17-milhao-de-moradores-diz-censo.shtml), which amounts to roughly 15% of the total city population. Though it's important to note that [not everyone who lives in a favela is mega poor](https://bmj.com.br/en/renda-aumenta-nas-favelas-e-classe-media-chega-a-65-dos-moradores/), it seems fair to assume that most favela dwellers are not (frequent) Uberers.

On the other end, the top 10% in Brazil earns an average of [R$8,034/month](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1294770/average-income-by-percentile-brazil/) (1,354€), which is more than enough for frequent ubering. Note how that isn't actually that much, and at the same time, it is also 11 times more than the average income of the bottom half, earning R$713/month, or around 120€. 
Unfortunately, I didn't find proper data to gain a solid idea of income distribution in São Paulo, and I reckon it must be sort of a tricky issue–say, who do you include for a truly representative sample, what sources of income, what ways of transferring money, etc. Likely, the top 1% does not even factor into those numbers for those reasons given. Nonetheless, [80% of the adult population of Brazil has already used the app at least once](https://www.uber.com/br/pt-br/newsroom/mais-de-120-milhoes-de-usuarios-e-5-milhoes-de-parceiros-uber-revela-dados-ineditos-sobre-seu-impacto-no-pais/) (according to Uber itself). Which is great–I guess?

It seems also kinda necessary, for example to make up for the faults in the public transport system. Another (probably *the*) main reason for hailing rides in São Paulo is security. Conversely, those that cannot afford to do so are either more exposed to these dangers–or, arguably, stigmatized as their cause. Effectively, they are restricted from certain spaces and modes of living.

In any case, a huge share of society is economically excluded from regular use. Crucially, those that have frequent access routinely save *hours* every day–hours in which poorer urban residents are locked to public transport.

## Income-mediated space-time

Situating Uber ride(r)s like this serves as a good reminder to how not everyone experiences (urban) time and space in the same way. The capacity to move around the city is not evenly distributed.

Some are, quasi by birth, literally restricted to walking speed: Barefooted bodies that, nakedly exposed to the violence of the streets, drag by force of their own muscles carts that serve to generate what may hardly be called an income.

On the opposite end of this spectrum, other bodies traverse the city so fast that they never actually become visible to the public: Shielded and hidden away by metal doors, the helicopters that transport them from one skyscraper to the next do away with streets entirely. In this reality, traffic does not exist. Instead, rotors cut through the air and open pathways that exist only for that moment, for those individuals. It is a reality so far removed from the lives below that the fact that it is heard much more than seen appears oddly fitting: The thunder that reverberates between concrete walls down here is the only thing that bridges the otherwise discontinuous gap between this world of urban mortals with that of the urban gods up above.

The spread of these severed worlds along a vertical axis somewhat resembles [The Politics of Verticality](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/article-801jsp/). Though urban class inequality follows logics that are surely very different from late-modern colonial occupation, both share a violently differential access to space and movement, leading to a curious "overlapping of two separate geographies that inhabit the same landscape" ([Mbembe, 2003](https://monoskop.org/File:Mbembe_Achille_2003_Necropolitics.pdf)).
